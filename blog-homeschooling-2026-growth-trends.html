📈 Why Homeschooling Keeps Growing in 2026

The short version

Homeschooling is now the fastest-growing form of K-12 education in the United States, with roughly 6% of school-age kids learning at home. Enrollment is climbing at nearly 5% a year on average across reporting states — years after the pandemic-era spike that first pushed a lot of families toward it, which tells you this isn't a temporary blip. It's a genuine, ongoing shift in how American families are choosing to educate their kids.

Which states are seeing the biggest jumps

Growth isn't even across the country. States like South Carolina, Vermont, Ohio, and New Hampshire are reporting some of the largest increases in homeschool participation. That spread is worth noticing — this isn't confined to one region or one type of state; it's showing up in places with very different politics, costs of living, and existing school systems, which suggests the reasons families are choosing this route vary a lot from state to state rather than boiling down to one single cause.

Who's actually homeschooling now

The stereotype of homeschooling as one narrow demographic doesn't hold up well against current data. Research on today's homeschooling families describes them as increasingly diverse — spanning a wide range of racial, ethnic, and secular backgrounds, not just the religious-homeschool image a lot of people still default to. If you're new to homeschooling and worried you don't fit some assumed mold, the actual population of families doing this now is much broader than that.

The regulatory fight that comes with the growth

Fast growth is also creating real friction. Lawmakers in states including Michigan and Illinois have pushed for stricter oversight and registration requirements for homeschool families, and those efforts are running into heavy pushback from parental-rights groups. This is very much an active, unsettled debate rather than a solved question — the rules that apply to your family depend heavily on your specific state, and those rules are genuinely in motion in several states right now, not fixed and stable.

What this means for public school districts

The flip side of families opting out is districts having to adjust. Public school systems in many areas are dealing with real funding and resource shifts as more families choose home-based or hybrid learning instead of traditional enrollment — school funding in most states is tied directly to enrollment counts, so a steady outflow of students has real budget consequences for the schools those families are leaving, which is part of why this keeps showing up as a political and community debate, not just an individual-family decision.

Where to go from here

If you're actually considering homeschooling rather than just reading about the trend, the practical next questions are the same ones we cover in our guide to choosing a free homeschool curriculum — what structure fits your kid, what "free" actually includes, and what your specific state requires. National trend data is useful context, but your state's actual registration and reporting rules are what will determine your day-to-day requirements, and those are worth checking directly rather than assuming based on what a neighboring state does.
